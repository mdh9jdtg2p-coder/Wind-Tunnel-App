Method. D2Q9 lattice-Boltzmann with a hybrid recursive-regularized collision, added bulk viscosity and a Vreman sub-grid model, run on the graphics processor. The sub-grid model is switched off next to the model wherever the grid resolves the laminar boundary layer. The aerofoil surface uses interpolated (Bouzidi) bounce-back, forces come from momentum exchange, and absorbing layers at both ends stop pressure waves reflecting round the tunnel. Free-stream turbulence comes from a divergence-free forcing strip inside the contraction, which acts like a turbulence grid. Smoke and heat are carried on a grid twice as fine using MacCormack advection.

Pressure. The lattice method is slightly compressible, so its raw pressure carries weak sound waves. The pressure field you see is the incompressible pressure, solved from the instantaneous velocity field with a multigrid Poisson solver, the way pressure is recovered from PIV data. Within 0.1 chord of the model it blends into the solver's own pressure, where the grid is finest.

Data reduction. In the closed section, coefficients are corrected for solid blockage, wake blockage and streamline curvature (Allen and Vincenti), as a real tunnel's data would be. Raw values are shown under each reading. Surface pressures come from the wall by a limited three-point fit along the surface normal, with the stagnation tap placed at the highest pressure round the nose. Static and dynamic pressure are read from a pitot-static probe 0.7 chord from the contraction, time-averaged over two acoustic transits.

Probes and recordings. Probes are ideal point sensors. They sample velocity and the solver's static pressure from the full grid at a fixed step interval, set under Probes (200 samples per flow-through by default), and are normalised by the pitot-static reference. While recording, the same samples drive the forces and the running sums behind the time-averaged and fluctuation views, so those statistics do not depend on how many frames are kept. A recording also stores frames for playback: velocity and pressure at 50 cells per chord, and vorticity, smoke and heat at up to 900 pixels across. The statistics are also kept on that finer grid, so the averaged pictures resolve the boundary layers. It stays on this device, compressed, with a checkpoint every 45 seconds (longer on very large grids) so an interrupted run can resume. Playback speed is a fraction of real tunnel time. Every CSV export starts with header lines giving the test conditions, sample rate and normalisation.

Flaps. Plain, split, single-slotted and Fowler trailing-edge flaps can be fitted to any NACA section at least 6% thick, and moved while the tunnel runs. A plain flap hinges on the camber line with a rounded knuckle; a split flap is a thin plate hinged on the lower surface; slotted and Fowler flaps are separate elements carried out of a cove under the main wing's shroud, leaving a slot about 2% of the chord wide, and a Fowler flap also slides back to add wing area. Coefficients use the retracted chord. The flap's share of the forces is summed exactly over the wall links that end on the flap, against the free-stream static pressure read at the pitot-static; its lift and drag are wall-corrected with the same factors as the totals. Slots and plates are thin: a slot 1.8% of the chord is about 2.7 cells across on the Fine grid, and refining it to 4.5 cells lowered lift by 5–7% for every flap type, so results are not fully grid-converged; on the Coarse grid the slot is effectively closed. Pressure-chart stations are along the stowed chord line with the model as it sits, so a deflected flap ends short of x/c = 1 and a Fowler flap beyond it. The flap types rank differently from a full-size wing. Full-scale tests (NACA TR 664; Abbott and von Doenhoff, ch. 8) give a 25% split flap at 45° roughly ΔCL 0.9–1.0 and a slotted flap at 35° about 1.3. Here, at Reynolds numbers in the thousands, the split flap gives more: NACA 2412 at 4°, 150 cells per chord, raw CL in a closed 4c×3c tunnel: clean 0.21, plain 30° 1.37, slotted 35° 1.47, Fowler 35° 1.72, split 45° 2.10; at 20° slotted 1.00 against split 1.15. Checks rule out the obvious artefacts: in a nearly unconfined 8c×8c tunnel (c/h 0.125) clean was 0.19, split 45° 1.73 (ΔCL 1.54) and slotted 35° 1.25 (ΔCL 1.06), and on a finer grid (250 cells per chord) to 1.96 and 1.39, keeping the same order each time. The likely cause is the laminar, thick boundary layers at these Reynolds numbers: the flow over the slotted flap appears to separate from much of its upper surface (the flap carries only about 15% of the lift, and on the coarse grid its upper-surface pressure stays flat), while a split flap works through the separated wake behind it, like a large Gurney flap, which is known to stay effective at low Reynolds number. Treat this as an interpretation, not a validated result: the gap was the same at Re 25,000 (slotted 35° 1.58, split 45° 2.21), but that is still far below the 105–106 where boundary layers turn turbulent, so it does not test the explanation either way.

Images and video. Save image draws the current view, or a recorded run's frame, time-averaged or fluctuation field, at up to 3840 pixels wide as PNG or JPEG, with a caption giving the model, Reynolds number, test conditions and colour scale. Save video renders a recorded run at the chosen playback speed and frame rate and encodes it in the browser as MP4 (H.264 where the browser offers it, otherwise VP9), which needs a current Chrome, Edge or Safari, or Firefox on a computer.

Scaling. The Reynolds number comes from your air speed and model chord, like a real rig. The default is a small smoke tunnel: a 60 mm model at 1.6 m/s, Re 6,400, with 0.15% free-stream turbulence. The grid resolves up to about 9,000 at Coarse, 18,000 at Standard, 36,000 at Ultra and 54,000 at Extreme (300 cells per chord, for desktop graphics cards: the page estimates the memory and time before switching and falls back if the grid doesn't fit). Above that the solver holds the limit and flags the run as capped, so a full-size test at 25 m/s is simulated at a much lower Reynolds number than the real thing.
